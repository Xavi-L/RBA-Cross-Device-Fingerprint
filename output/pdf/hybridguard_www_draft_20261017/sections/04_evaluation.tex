\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\subsection{Research Questions and Materials}
We examine detection on controlled App modifications (RQ1), the contribution of semantic references through fixed-check studies (RQ2), normal-alarm and evidence-availability costs using historical normals (RQ3), and incremental evidence from paired App--browser observations (RQ4). Together, these comparisons examine when consistency supplies useful integrity evidence.

\paragraph{Controlled App benchmark.}
The 378 record positions cover 14 manipulation configurations, three repetitions, and three Android emulator environments (API 29, 30, and 36 with SwiftShader). Each admitted run contributes a normal pre-record, an effective modification, and a confirmed normal post-record: 126 modifications and 252 normals. Post-records serve evaluation and control, not earlier predictions. Three leave-one-environment-out configurations each use 252 training records (84 modified, 168 normal) and 126 held-out records (42 modified, 84 normal). Disjoint held-out records are aggregated once. Development use of these environments and recipes precludes interpreting this split as unseen-tool or independent physical-device attack generalization.

\paragraph{Historical normal records.}
The mobile test-cloud (MTC) material comprises 891 representative records: 630 discovery records for constraints and tree training, 144 historical development records, and 117 historically reserved evaluation records. Representatives are manufacturer/model/Android-release combinations, not independent physical devices. The latter groups remain separately reported; applying three models to the same records does not triple their number. Normal means absence of the study-defined target manipulation, without certifying authenticity or absence of automation and debugging software.

\paragraph{Specialist and paired material.}
Memory contributes 18 effective modifications and 48 normals, with six additional operations having no observable effect. Timezone contributes six modifications and 30 normals, including six normal system changes. Geometry contributes six modifications and 66 normals, including six normal layout changes. The system and layout changes remain subsets of their normal denominators. App--browser material comprises 60 paired positions, with 14 modifications and 46 normals: 18 pilot and 42 matched positions, including 12 and 34 normals, respectively. These materials have distinct roles and development histories; their denominators are not pooled into an accuracy or independent-device population.

\paragraph{Methods and measures.}
\full combines within-view and cross-view checks. Four ablations rebuild their own sparse and retention stages: no MTC alarm cap, Web-only, no memory relation, and no timezone relation. Removing the cap relaxes only that alarm budget, retaining MTC data, assessability, coverage, and other normal constraints. Web-only changes both information and constructible relations, precluding a pure single-factor causal interpretation. Fixed specialist checks and \full+C1 retain separate identities.

The App tree uses the same fold-specific admitted candidate-state representation, with T/F/U indicators, maximum depth three, minimum leaf size two, and Gini splitting. Training weights allocate one half to modifications, one quarter to controlled normals, and one quarter to MTC normals; modification weight is balanced across configurations. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix} gives further settings. This comparison fixes representation and capacity, without testing the strongest classifier over raw fields. Rules use saved \texttt{RETENTION} outputs; the tree uses saved \texttt{FITTED} outputs.

A detection is an alarm on an effective modification. Normal alarms are reported separately. Separate counts retain \hgstate{F} for no alert, \hgstate{U} for unknown, \hgstate{FAILED} for failure, and \texttt{EMPTY\_MODEL} for an empty detector. Unknowns remain in their denominators and count as neither normal decisions nor successful rejections. Comparisons are descriptive, without new significance tests, confidence intervals, or population estimates. This revision uses saved results without new collection, fitting, selection, prediction, or timing.

\subsection{RQ1: Can Cross-Layer Consistency Expose Manipulated Web Reports?}

\full detects 105/126 effective modifications (83.3\%), with no alarms on 252 controlled normal records. This establishes detection capability on the benchmark, although its mixture of within-view and cross-view checks prevents attributing all 105 detections to cross-layer relations.

Table~\ref{tab:app} compares the alternatives. Web-only and the ablation without memory each detect 96/126, losing nine resource detections. Removing the MTC cap raises detection to 108/126 through three additional screen detections, whose normal cost is examined in Section~\ref{sec:eval_cost}. The fixed App tree detects 72/126, characterizing this representation and capacity rather than a general advantage of rules over machine learning.

All methods miss nine webdriver-only and nine WebGL-identifier modifications. Historical raw WebGL unavailability and loss of original webdriver-false semantics prevent corresponding candidates from meeting assessability requirements. These misses identify limits of the admitted representation, without establishing intrinsic undetectability.

The table groups 14 configurations into five groups using their complete state-count vectors across all six methods. Each original configuration retains nine modifications, with group sizes preserving weight. Equal counts do not establish identical record-level outputs. Aggregate detection establishes capability; the next comparisons explain what references contribute.

\subsection{RQ2: Why Do Cross-Layer References Help?}
\label{sec:eval_references}
\input{tables/02_mechanisms}
Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms} compares fixed checks, distinct from complete-detector ablations. The three cohorts retain separate denominators and test complementary roles for references: additional device context, interpretation of legitimate co-change, and container context.

\paragraph{Memory: additional device context.}
This study asks whether a Native bound exposes reports accepted by a global Web threshold. The Web-only $>8$ GiB condition detects 6/18 modifications, while the Native memory reference detects 18/18; both have 0/48 normal alarms. The additional 12 detections are upward changes to 4 and 8 GiB. Both detect the six changes to 16 GiB. Six operations targeting 2 GiB have no observable effect and trigger neither check; they belong to neither the effective-modification denominator nor the 48 normals. Native memory supplies device-specific context absent from the global threshold. This supports the tested upward comparisons, without evaluating downward, within-envelope, or coordinated modifications. These 12 additional specialist detections are separate from the nine resource detections lost in the App benchmark.

\paragraph{Timezone: interpreting legitimate co-change.}
Here the question is whether a reference distinguishes report modification from a normal setting change while retaining detection. Native-reference and absolute-offset checks both detect 6/6 modifications, but produce 0/30 and 6/30 normal alarms, respectively. All six alarms arise from normal system timezone changes. The reference interprets the tested offsets moving together, whereas the absolute condition alarms on that movement. Thus, removing the relation without changing the main benchmark's detection total does not establish its redundancy. Separately reported complete-model replay supports this distinction: no-timezone and Web-only also alarm on those six normal changes, while \full produces no alarms.

\paragraph{Geometry: contextual evidence from the Host.}
The geometry study asks whether container context separates layout changes from report changes. The fixed Web-height threshold detects 6/6 modifications but alarms on 6/66 normals. The same-Web viewport relation detects 0/6 with 0/66 normal alarms. The Host geometry bound detects 6/6 with 0/66 normal alarms, distinguishing the tested changes while retaining detection. The same-Web result shows why low normal alarms alone do not establish informative checking.

The Host check remains an independent specialist and is not integrated into \full. \full configurations 01/02 still use the old height condition and alarm on the six normal layout changes; configuration 03 neither detects the six modifications nor alarms on those changes. The Host result cannot replace these complete-model outcomes. Across these studies, references supply useful context under tested conditions; the comparisons do not establish effectiveness for every cross-layer check.

\subsection{RQ3: What Does Robust Integrity Checking Cost?}
\label{sec:eval_cost}
\input{tables/03_normal_cap}
The normal-record comparisons examine two costs: alarms on legitimate observations and loss of definite decisions. Table~\ref{tab:cap} places the gain from 105/126 to 108/126 detections against alarms on all 144 historical development and all 117 historical reserved evaluation records after removing the cap. With the cap, configurations 01/02 each produce one alarm in each group; configuration 03 produces none. Both methods still have 0/252 controlled-normal alarms. Those controlled normals therefore fail to expose the much larger historical-normal cost.

Saved cap-free models select broad conditions, including hardware concurrency $>1$ and language-list length $>1$. They satisfy the controlled-normal budget while covering many MTC normals. This reveals insufficient coverage of normal conditions by the controlled set, beyond a modest threshold tradeoff. On the separate 630 training normals, capped configurations produce 6, 6, and 0 alarms, respectively, with 51 unknowns each; uncapped configurations alarm on all 630. These training outcomes are not added to either evaluation denominator, nor are model outputs pooled as extra records.

Evidence availability imposes another cost. Each \full configuration has 8/144 and 6/117 unknowns, or 14/261 within that configuration. Removing the memory reference reduces this to 1/261 but loses resource detections. Binary tree outputs do not recover missing measurements or establish more complete evidence. Unknowns are not successful rejections, and low alarms do not imply high definite-output coverage. Detection gains must therefore be read with normal alarms and evidence availability; these constraints do not establish general deployment robustness.

\subsection{RQ4: Does an Additional Observation Surface Add Integrity Evidence?}
\input{tables/04_paired}
Table~\ref{tab:paired} tests the increment from the retained C1 condition on 60 paired positions. For App timezone, App language, Browser timezone, and Browser language, \full detects 2/2, 0/2, 0/5, and 0/5, respectively. \full+C1 gives 2/2, 0/2, 5/5, and 0/5: totals rise from 2/14 to 7/14. All five additional detections concern Browser timezone; neither language family gains detection.

Both methods have 0/46 normal alarms. Counts agree across three frozen configurations and are displayed once; they neither become 21/42 nor establish independent replications or identical record-level outputs. Historical normals reveal an availability cost with unchanged alarm counts: unknowns rise from 8/144 to 11/144 in historical development and remain 6/117 in historical reserved evaluation. Within each configuration, the combined count thus rises from 14/261 to 17/261.

C1 has no timestamp gate, so 0/46 does not establish general robustness to asynchronous normal setting changes. The evidence supports a targeted increment from another observation surface, without establishing complete cross-endpoint detection or an automatic benefit from more views.

\subsection{Cross-RQ Findings}
The comparisons identify three linked findings. First, references can supply context that isolated Web checks lack: Native memory distinguishes tested reports accepted by a global threshold, and Host geometry supplies container information beyond the same-Web relation. Second, interpreting legitimate co-change can preserve detection while reducing particular normal alarms. The timezone and geometry comparisons show why this value can be hidden by an unchanged modification-detection total.

Third, gains depend on the relation's information, applicability, and available evidence. The same-Web geometry misses, historical alarm costs, and additional C1 unknowns make these dependencies observable. In these comparisons, the useful references provide measured context that distinguishes the reports at issue; adding a view alone does not settle that question. Taken together, these results support a conditional mechanism interpretation for the studied materials, rather than a universal integrity criterion. Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses the resulting limits on attribution, generality, and integration.
